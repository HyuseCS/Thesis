# Data Collection and Simulator Validity (revised 2026-10-03: Hojaji 2024 main dataset + external test set)

### Data Collection

This thesis uses two datasets: a main dataset from Hojaji et al. (2024), used to build and test the method, and an external test set recorded for this thesis, used only to check whether the trained surrogate holds for new drivers, a newer version of the simulator and different hardware.

**Main dataset (Hojaji et al., 2024).** The main dataset is the segment-specific behavioural dataset published as supplementary material with Hojaji et al. (2024) under a Creative Commons CC BY 4.0 licence (the article states that "the main datasets have been included as supplementary files"; the channels and metrics are described in its Tables A1 and A2). It holds 826 laps from 166 drivers in Assetto Corsa Competizione (ACC), all driving the McLaren 720S GT3 at Brands Hatch, recorded between October 2022 and January 2023. In that study all drivers used the same track, vehicle setup and game setup (Hojaji et al., 2024). Each lap is already divided into 19 microsectors (9 corners and 10 straights, with the start/finish straight split into its start and end parts). For each microsector the dataset gives its time, seven summary statistics (minimum, maximum, mean, start, end, change and standard deviation) for each telemetry channel, and eight driver-input KPIs: brake length, brake duration, trail-braking duration, throttle duration, throttle application speed, throttle release, peak steering value and time to peak steering. Because the speed at the start of each microsector equals the speed at the end of the previous one, entry and exit speeds can be read directly. Drivers contributed between 1 and 8 laps each (median 5).

The dataset needs light cleaning before Step 1. One microsector name spelled two ways ("Str 0-1(End)" and "Str 0-1 (End)") is unified. All laps of one driver (D_045) are removed, because their lap times do not match the sum of their microsector times (differences of 35 to 46 s). Four laps that start from a standstill (entry speed into the first microsector below 60 km/h; P_001, P_010, P_052 and P_062, all Lap 1) are removed as out-laps, since their normal lap times would let them pass the |z| > 3 filter.

**External test set (recorded for this thesis).** To test the surrogate outside the data it was built on, [N drivers] recruited drivers each record [laps per driver] laps of the McLaren 720S GT3 at Brands Hatch in ACC [version] on their own rigs at home. The software and in-game settings are fixed and sent to every driver as an instruction sheet with the car setup file; the hardware is not fixed, so each driver reports it on a short form (Appendix 1). The minimum requirement is a steering wheel with pedals. Telemetry is exported with ACC's built-in MoTeC logging (Vasilakos, 2019) and processed with the same MoTeC i2 Pro workspace, maths channels and export settings as the main dataset [MoTeC instructions, Appendix X], so that the same 19 microsectors and the same KPIs are produced. Drivers upload the raw MoTeC files (.ld and .ldx) and the form to [upload location].

These laps are never used for training, feature selection or tuning. Because they differ from the main dataset in simulator version, hardware and drivers, they show how far the surrogate's accuracy carries over to new conditions. Hardware is recorded because force-feedback settings can change lap time and some steering measures (Murphy et al., 2026) and a limited field of view can affect perceived speed (Kemeny & Panerai, 2003).

### Simulator Validity and Scope

A simulator should not be labelled "valid" or "invalid" as a whole. Its validity depends on the research question and the variables measured (Himmels et al., 2023). Absolute and relative validity both describe how a simulator compares with real driving, and this study makes no such comparison. It does not claim that ACC telemetry is equivalent to real-car telemetry. Its context is sim racing as an esport: the drivers, laps, fast reference and optimized targets all exist inside ACC, and every comparison is made between laps recorded in the same simulator with the same car and track. The results are therefore stated for competitive sim racing in ACC, not for professional real-world racing. The wider evidence on simulator validity, including studies suggesting that simulator measures partly reflect real driving skill, is reviewed in Chapter 2 (Section 2.2).

**Consistency of the measurements.** Separately from validity, the study checks how consistent each KPI is across a driver's recorded laps, using [reliability statistic, e.g. intraclass correlation]. KPIs with low consistency are reported as a limitation.

### Threats to Validity and Mitigations

| Threat | Effect | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| No motion or sustained G-force cues [if the rig has no motion platform] | Drivers may misjudge speed, and cornering and speed KPIs may shift (Reymond et al., 2001; Godley et al., 2002; Kemeny & Panerai, 2003) | Make no absolute claims about real cars; record each driver's display and motion setup |
| Braking may differ from real driving | Some braking measures agreed with a test track, but simulator drivers braked more abruptly. Absolute peak-brake values may not match real cars (Lee et al., 2002) | Compare braking KPIs between drivers and laps, not with real-car values |
| Force-feedback and hardware settings | Force-feedback settings changed lap time and some steering measures (Murphy et al., 2026). Effects of the wheel model itself are assumed by extension | Minimum hardware (wheel and pedals); wheel rotation matched to the car; record each driver's hardware and force-feedback settings; report test error by hardware group where possible |
| Skill mix of the sample | Skill strongly affects control KPIs (van Leeuwen et al., 2017), so pooled results depend on who is recruited | Main dataset spans 166 drivers; report the skill distribution by fastest lap (Joyce et al., 2024) for both datasets |
| Learning effects across a session | Later laps may be faster because of practice rather than skill | External test set: [k] warm-up laps, exported but not used, checked for levelling off (Ronen & Yair, 2013); lap order kept |
| Simulator sickness | Performance may get worse (Klüver et al., 2016; road dual-task study) | Self-report on the driver form; exclude sessions where sickness is reported |
| Simulated vehicle and track model | Validity varies by simulator and measure, and speed validity was inconsistent (Wynne et al., 2019; Himmels et al., 2024) | Keep all conditions the same; interpret results only inside ACC |
| Export and logging errors | Laps may be missing, overwritten or corrupted (Hojaji et al., 2024) | Check the export and saved-lap setting in [ACC version] (Vasilakos, 2019); apply the Hojaji et al. (2026) cleaning rules |
| Secondary data with partly unknown settings | The exact ACC version and in-game settings of the main dataset are not stored in the files | Cite the dataset and its protocol (Hojaji et al., 2024); confirm settings with the authors where possible; use the external test set to check how results carry over to a newer version |
| Unsupervised remote sessions (external test set) | Drivers may use the wrong settings, or someone else may drive | Instruction sheet and setup file; settings screenshot; check car and track in every file; one driver per session declared on the form |
| Generalization to real cars | Real racing adds physical load and psycho-emotional stress (Holland et al., 2025) | Out of scope; results are stated for sim racing only |
